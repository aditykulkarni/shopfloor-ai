Customers use "AI" to mean anything from an Excel macro to a chatbot. This session gives you one map you can draw on a whiteboard, so every later session has a place to sit.

Rules versus learning

Every piece of software turns inputs into outputs. The question is who writes the rule. In traditional software a person writes it: "if the torque exceeds 60 N·m, raise an alarm". In machine learning the computer finds the rule from examples: you show it ten thousand past readings, each labelled "failed" or "did not fail", and it works out which combinations of torque, speed and temperature come before a failure.

That is the whole idea. Machine learning is useful when the rule exists but is too complicated, too fuzzy or too changeable for a person to write down, and when you have enough labelled history for the computer to find it.

The four nested circles

TermWhat it meansManufacturing example
Artificial intelligenceAny system that does something we would call intelligent if a person did it. Includes hand-written rules.An expert system that checks a weld schedule against a rule book
Machine learningSystems that learn patterns from data instead of being given rules.Predicting machine failure from sensor history
Deep learningMachine learning with neural networks of many layers. Very good with images, sound and text.Spotting cracks in castings from camera images
Generative AIDeep learning models that produce new content: text, images, code, 3D shapes.A copilot that drafts a work instruction from a process plan

Each circle sits inside the one above. Generative AI is a kind of deep learning, which is a kind of machine learning, which is a kind of AI. When a customer asks "do you have AI?", it helps to ask which circle they mean.

What kind of data, what kind of AI

The type of data decides the type of model far more than the industry does. Manufacturing has all five kinds:

  • Tables: BOMs, routings, quality records, ERP transactions. Classic machine learning (Part 2) works best here.
  • Time series: sensor streams from machines, energy meters, test rigs. Machine learning with features built from time windows.
  • Images and video: inspection cameras, weld seams, drawings. Deep learning (Part 3).
  • Text: change notices, work instructions, service reports, RFPs. Large language models (Parts 4 and 5).
  • 3D geometry and simulation: CAD models, meshes, factory flow models. Generative design and simulation surrogates (Part 3 and the capstone).

Three things AI needs, in this order

  1. A decision worth improving. Someone has to act differently because of the output: stop a machine, reject a part, approve a change faster.
  2. Data that describes past cases. Enough of it, correct enough, and connected to the outcome you care about.
  3. A model. Usually the easiest part, and often a standard one.

Most failed AI projects start at step 3. Most successful ones start at step 1. You will see this pattern in almost every case study in this course.

In your job

When a prospect asks "what do you have for AI?", answer with a question from step 1: "Which decision costs you the most today?" A design manager will say change approvals. A plant head will say unplanned downtime. A quality head will say escapes to the customer. Each answer points to a different circle on the map and a different data source on your platform.

A plant writes a rule: "if spindle temperature exceeds 85 °C, stop the machine". Is this machine learning?

  1. Yes, because a computer applies it
  2. No, because a person wrote the rule rather than the computer learning it from data
  3. Yes, because it uses sensor data

Machine learning means the rule is learned from examples. A hand-written threshold is ordinary automation, which still counts as AI only in the widest sense.

Which of these is generative AI?

  1. A model that classifies castings as OK or defective
  2. A model that predicts remaining tool life in minutes
  3. A model that drafts a first version of a work instruction

Generative models produce new content. Classifying and predicting a number are discriminative tasks.

The line {p["part_no"]: p for p in parts} builds a dictionary from the list in one go. It is called a comprehension. Read it as "for every p in parts, use its part number as the key and the whole record as the value".

Step 5: a function that walks the tree

def children(parent_no):
    return [p for p in parts if p["parent"] == parent_no]

def show(part_no, depth=0):
    p = by_no[part_no]
    print("    " * depth + f'{p["part_no"]}  {p["description"]}  x{p["qty"]}')
    for child in children(part_no):
        show(child["part_no"], depth + 1)

show("E3-1000")

You should see the chassis with the frame and the wheel assembly indented under it, and their parts indented again. show calls itself for each child. That is recursion, and it is the natural way to walk any tree: a BOM, a folder structure, a factory hierarchy.

The f'...' string is an f-string. Anything in curly brackets is replaced by its value.

You know BOMs as an engineer. This session looks at them as a data scientist would, because every AI use case in PLM starts by reading product structures into a table.

One line, two parts

The basic record of a BOM is not a part. It is a relationship: parent P uses child C, quantity Q. Store a BOM as one row per relationship and you get what database people call an adjacency list:

parentchild (part_no)qty
E3-1000 ChassisE3-1100 Main frame weldment1
E3-1100 Main frame weldmentE3-9001 Bolt M10x4016
E3-2000 PowertrainE3-9001 Bolt M10x408

The bolt appears twice because two assemblies use it. So a BOM is not a tree in the strict sense; it is a graph in which a part can have many parents. Where-used queries ("which assemblies use this bolt?") follow the graph upwards. Explosions ("what goes into the chassis?") follow it downwards.

Keys

A key is the column, or set of columns, that identifies a row uniquely. In the table above, part_no alone is not unique; the pair (parent, part_no) is. Real PLM systems add more: revision, effectivity dates and variant conditions. Getting the key wrong is the most common reason two systems disagree about the "same" BOM.

Levels

The level of a line is its distance from the top. The vehicle is level 0, the chassis level 1, the frame level 2, the bolt level 3. Indented BOM reports are just the rows sorted by structure and indented by level. You computed this last unit by walking up through parents.

EBOM, MBOM and BOP as three tables

  • EBOM: structure by function, as engineering designed it. Rows: parent assembly, child part, quantity.
  • MBOM: structure by how the plant builds it. Adds phantom and kit levels, plant-specific parts (consumables, packaging), and can reorganise assemblies by station.
  • Bill of process: operations, their sequence, stations, times, and which MBOM parts each operation consumes.

Most "digital thread" questions are joins between these three tables. "Which operations are affected if this EBOM part changes?" means: find the part in the EBOM, find the matching MBOM lines, find the operations that consume them.

Tidy data

Data is tidy when each row is one observation, each column one variable, each table one kind of thing. A spreadsheet MBOM with merged cells, colour coding and totals in the middle is not tidy. Before any analysis, and certainly before any machine learning, you reshape data into tidy tables. That reshaping is usually most of the work.

In your job

When you scope an AI project, ask for an export of the BOM as one row per parent-child relationship with keys. How long it takes the customer to produce that file tells you more about their data readiness than any questionnaire.

Why is part_no alone not a valid key for BOM lines?

  1. Part numbers contain letters
  2. The same part can appear under several parents
  3. Keys must be numbers

A shared part such as a bolt has one row per parent, so you need the parent in the key too.

"Which assemblies use bolt E3-9001?" is a:

  1. Where-used query, going up the graph
  2. BOM explosion, going down the graph
  3. Routing query

Where-used starts at a child and finds its parents.

Which is the best description of tidy data?

  1. Data with no missing values
  2. Data stored in one big spreadsheet tab
  3. One observation per row, one variable per column, one kind of thing per table

Tidy is about shape, not cleanliness. Clean but badly shaped data is still hard to analyse.

The situation

Two weeks after your first meeting, Priya Deshmukh at Sahyadri Motors sends you a request: "We want AI to generate MBOMs automatically from the EBOM. My planners spend too long on it." She attaches a sample: the EBOM and MBOM for one vehicle variant, exported to Excel.

FindingLines
EBOM parts with no matching MBOM line212

Priya's team is 12 planners. She estimates each new variant takes 6 planner-days to build into an MBOM, and Sahyadri launches about 40 variants a year. Fully loaded, a planner costs ₹18 lakh a year (about 220 working days). Separately, the plant reports roughly 25 line stoppages a year caused by "wrong part at station" issues, each costing about ₹4 lakh in lost output and rework.

Your questions

  1. What do the four findings tell you about whether an AI model could learn to generate MBOMs from this data?
  2. Estimate the annual cost of MBOM creation and of BOM-related line stoppages. Show your arithmetic.
  3. What would you propose to Priya as a first step, and how would you position it so it does not sound like "no"?
  4. How would you describe the long-term AI opportunity once the first step is done?
Model answer

1. A model learns from examples of EBOM-to-MBOM transformations. Here the examples are unreliable: 1,270 MBOM lines (55%) point to an old EBOM revision, so the pairs do not describe the same product. The 212 missing and 390 unmatched parts may be legitimate manufacturing additions or errors, and nobody can yet tell which. A model trained on this would learn the mess as well as the method. Data first.

2. MBOM creation: 40 variants × 6 days = 240 planner-days. A planner-day costs ₹18 lakh ÷ 220 ≈ ₹8,200, so about ₹20 lakh a year. Line stoppages: 25 × ₹4 lakh = ₹1 crore a year. The stoppages cost five times more than the planning effort. That changes the story: the value is in accuracy, not only speed.

3.
