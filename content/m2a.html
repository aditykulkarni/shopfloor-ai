Nearly all industrial machine learning is supervised learning on tables. Learn its five ideas well and you can question any vendor's model, including your own.

Idea 1: features and a label

Each row is one example: one machine at one moment, one part, one pack. The features are what you know at the time of the decision (temperature, torque, tool wear). The label is what you want to predict (did it fail within the next shift?). The model learns a function from features to label.

If the label is a category (fail or not, OK or defective) it is classification. If it is a number (minutes of tool life left, kilograms of scrap) it is regression.

Idea 2: you cannot test on what you trained on

A model can memorise its training rows. So you hold some rows back, usually 20 to 30%, train on the rest and measure on the held-back test set. The test score estimates how the model will do on data it has never seen, which is the only score that matters.

Idea 3: overfitting and underfitting

A model that is too simple misses real patterns (underfitting: poor on training and test data). A model that is too flexible learns noise (overfitting: excellent on training data, poor on test data). The gap between training and test scores is your warning light. Every model has knobs, such as tree depth, that trade one off against the other.

Idea 4: accuracy lies when failures are rare

In the AI4I dataset you will use, about 3.4% of rows are failures. A "model" that always says "no failure" is 96.6% accurate and completely useless. For rare events, use the confusion matrix:

Predicted failurePredicted OK
Actual failureTrue positive (TP): caughtFalse negative (FN): missed
Actual OKFalse positive (FP): false alarmTrue negative (TN)
  • Recall = TP ÷ (TP + FN): of the real failures, how many did we catch?
  • Precision = TP ÷ (TP + FP): of our alarms, how many were real?
  • F1 = the harmonic mean of the two, a single number when you need one.

A maintenance manager cares about recall (missed failures stop the line) and precision (too many false alarms and the team stops listening). Which matters more is a business decision, not a technical one.

Idea 5: leakage

Leakage is when a feature contains information that would not be available at decision time, often a disguised version of the label. In AI4I, the columns TWF, HDF, PWF, OSF and RNF record which failure mode happened. Using them to predict "machine failure" gives near-perfect scores and a model that is worthless in production. Leakage is the most common reason a pilot looks brilliant and then fails.

In your job

When anyone shows you a model with 99% accuracy, ask three questions: what share of the data is the positive class, what are precision and recall on a held-out test set, and could any feature be known only after the event? Those three questions catch most overclaimed results.

The situation

Godavari Foods is a fictional packaged-snacks maker with eight form-fill-seal packaging lines at its Nashik plant. Unplanned stoppages on the lines cost about ₹45,000 an hour in lost output, and each line averages 60 hours of unplanned downtime a year, mostly from sealing-jaw and film-drive faults.

A start-up ran a three-month predictive maintenance pilot on two lines. Its summary slide says: "Our model is 99.1% accurate. Projected savings ₹2.2 crore a year across the plant." The plant head, Sunita Patil, forwards it to you and asks: "Should we roll this out? Your platform could host it."

You ask for the details. The pilot evaluated the model on 12,000 one-hour windows. 110 windows were followed by a stoppage. The model flagged 140 windows; 60 of them were followed by a stoppage. The start-up assumed every flagged stoppage would be fully avoided.

Your questions

  1. Build the confusion matrix and calculate recall and precision.
  2. Is "99.1% accurate" a meaningful number here? Compare it with always predicting "no stoppage".
  3. Re-estimate the savings with explicit assumptions. Where does the ₹2.2 crore come from, and is it credible?
  4. What would you advise Sunita, and how would you say it without attacking the start-up?
Model answer

1. TP = 60, FP = 140 − 60 = 80, FN = 110 − 60 = 50, TN = 12,000 − 60 − 80 − 50 = 11,810. Recall = 60 ÷ 110 = 55%. Precision = 60 ÷ 140 = 43%.

2. Accuracy = (60 + 11,810) ÷ 12,000 = 98.9%, close to their claim. Always predicting "no stoppage" scores 11,890 ÷ 12,000 = 99.1%. So the headline accuracy is no better than doing nothing. It says nothing about value.

3. Plant downtime cost: 8 lines × 60 hours × ₹45,000 = ₹2.16 crore a year, which is where ₹2.2 crore comes from. It assumes every hour of downtime is avoided. More realistic: the model catches 55% of stoppages, and an early warning might avoid perhaps half the downtime of a caught stoppage, because some faults still need a stop to fix. That gives about 55% × 50% × ₹2.16 crore ≈ ₹59 lakh a year, minus the cost of 80 false alarms per 110 stoppages (each an inspection, perhaps 30 minutes of a technician). Still possibly worth doing, but about a quarter of the claim.

4. Advise a second phase, not a roll-out: run the model live on two lines for three months, record every alert and what the technician found, and measure downtime per line against the other six lines. Position it as "protecting the result": "The pilot found a real signal. Before scaling, let us measure the savings the way your finance team will."

What the case teaches. Accuracy on rare events is meaningless, and savings claims usually assume 100% recall and 100% avoidance. Re-doing the arithmetic calmly, with the customer, builds more trust than any product claim.

One tree is easy to read and easy to fool. Many trees together are much harder to fool. Then the most important decision is not the model but the threshold, and that is a money decision.

Ensembles: many weak opinions

A random forest trains hundreds of trees, each on a random sample of rows and features, and lets them vote. Individual trees overfit in different ways, so their errors partly cancel. Gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM, scikit-learn's HistGradientBoosting) builds trees one after another, each correcting the previous ones' errors. On tabular data these two families win most practical problems. Deep learning rarely beats them on tables.

Probabilities, not just yes or no

Most classifiers output a probability: "this window has a 0.23 chance of ending in failure". The default rule turns anything above 0.5 into an alarm. Nothing makes 0.5 special. Lower the threshold and you catch more failures (higher recall) but raise more false alarms (lower precision). Raise it and the reverse happens.

Choose the threshold from costs

Put a price on each kind of error:

Total cost = FN × cost of a missed failure + FP × cost of a false alarm

If a missed failure costs ₹2 lakh (downtime, scrap, emergency repair) and a false alarm costs ₹5,000 (a technician's inspection), a miss is 40 times worse than a false alarm. The best threshold will be low, accepting many false alarms. If false alarms trigger a full line stop, the ratio changes and so does the threshold. Try every threshold on the test set and pick the one with the lowest total cost.

Imbalance

With 3% positives, a model can learn to ignore the rare class. Two simple fixes: tell the model to weight the rare class more (class_weight="balanced"), and judge it by precision, recall and cost rather than accuracy. Fancier resampling methods exist but rarely help as much as a good threshold.

Cross-validation

One test split can be lucky or unlucky. Cross-validation splits the data into, say, five parts, trains five times with a different part held out each time, and averages the scores. Use it to compare models fairly. Keep a final test set aside for the very end.

Feature engineering with physics

Engineers have an advantage here. Power is torque times angular speed. Overstrain relates to tool wear times torque. Heat dissipation depends on the temperature difference between process and air. Adding these as columns often helps a model more than switching algorithms, and makes it easier to explain.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("ai4i2020.csv")

def add_features(d):
    d = d.copy()
    d["temp_diff"] = d["Process temperature [K]"] - d["Air temperature [K]"]
    d["power_w"] = d["Torque [Nm]"] * d["Rotational speed [rpm]"] * 2 * np.pi / 60
    d["strain"] = d["Tool wear [min]"] * d["Torque [Nm]"]
    d["type_code"] = d["Type"].map({"L": 0, "M": 1, "H": 2})
    return d

df = add_features(df)

Step 4: choose the threshold by cost

from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix

COST_MISS = 200_000     # ₹ per missed failure: downtime, scrap, emergency repair
COST_ALARM = 5_000      # ₹ per false alarm: one technician inspection

rows = []
for t in np.arange(0.05, 0.95, 0.05):
    tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(y_test, (proba >= t).astype(int)).ravel()
    rows.append({"threshold": round(t, 2), "caught": tp, "missed": fn, "false_alarms": fp,
                 "recall": tp / (tp + fn), "precision": tp / (tp + fp) if tp + fp else 0,
                 "cost_rs": fn * COST_MISS + fp * COST_ALARM})
table = pd.DataFrame(rows)
best_row = table.loc[table["cost_rs"].idxmin()]
print(best_row)
table.round(3)

Read the table from top to bottom: as the threshold rises, false alarms fall and misses rise. The cheapest row is your operating point. Change COST_ALARM to ₹50,000 (as if every alarm stopped the line) and rerun: the best threshold moves up.

Step 6: the ROI sheet

def annual_value(recall, alarms_per_failure, failures_per_year=120, avoid_share=0.5,
                 cost_failure=200_000, cost_alarm=5_000, run_cost=600_000):
    caught = failures_per_year * recall
    saved = caught * avoid_share * cost_failure
    alarm_cost = caught * alarms_per_failure * cost_alarm
    return round(saved - alarm_cost - run_cost)

r = best_row
alarms_per_failure = r["false_alarms"] / max(r["caught"], 1)
print("Net value per year: ₹", annual_value(r["recall"], alarms_per_failure))

Every number here is an assumption you must state: 120 failures a year on the target line, half of each caught failure's cost avoided, ₹6 lakh a year to run the system. Change them and watch the answer move. That sensitivity is the honest part of an ROI sheet.
