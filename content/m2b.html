Trees are great for tables. For images, sound and text, neural networks took over in the 2010s. This session explains why, in words an engineering manager will follow.

A network is a stack of adjustable filters

A neural network is a long chain of simple calculations. Each layer takes numbers in, multiplies them by weights, adds them up and passes the result through a simple bend (if the sum is negative, output zero). With millions of weights and enough layers, the chain can represent almost any relationship between input and output.

Training means adjusting the weights. Show the network an image, compare its guess with the label, and nudge every weight slightly in the direction that would have made the guess less wrong. The method for computing those nudges is called backpropagation; the nudging is gradient descent. Repeat over thousands of images, many times. One full pass through the training data is an epoch.

Why images need convolutions

A 224 × 224 colour image is about 150,000 numbers. Connecting every pixel to every unit of the next layer would need billions of weights. A convolutional network instead slides small filters (3 × 3 pixels) across the image. Early filters learn to respond to edges and spots; later layers combine them into textures, then shapes, then objects. The same filter works anywhere in the image, which is exactly what you want when a crack can appear anywhere on a casting.

Transfer learning: the reason this is practical

Training a vision network from scratch needs millions of images. But networks trained on general photo collections (ImageNet) already know edges, textures and shapes. Fine-tuning takes such a pretrained network and retrains only its last layers, then gently all of them, on your images. A few hundred labelled images per class can be enough for a clear visual defect. This is why visual inspection pilots can start small.

What can go wrong

  • Shortcut learning. If every defective sample was photographed on a Tuesday under warmer light, the model may learn "warm light means defect". Check what the model looks at, and photograph good and bad parts in the same conditions.
  • Distribution shift. A new camera, a dirty lens or a new supplier's material changes the images. Accuracy drops silently. Monitor it.
  • Rare defects. If a defect type appears five times a year, you will not have enough examples. Anomaly detection helps: train only on good parts and flag anything that looks unusual.
  • Label disagreement. Two inspectors often disagree on borderline parts. If people cannot agree, the model cannot learn a sharp boundary. Measure inspector agreement first.

GPUs

Training multiplies huge grids of numbers, which graphics processors do thousands at a time. Kaggle and Colab give limited free GPU time, enough for this course. In production, inspection models usually run on a small edge computer next to the camera.

In your job

When a customer asks about visual inspection, ask four questions: how many images of each defect type exist, do inspectors agree on borderline cases, is lighting controlled, and what happens to a rejected part. Those answers decide feasibility more than the model choice does.

  2. Which defect types are good first targets for a vision model, and which are hard? Why?
  3. Deepa wants to replace all inspectors. What would you propose instead for the first phase?

Model answer

1. Panels a year: 9,000 × about 300 days = 2.7 million. Defective: 1.8% ≈ 48,600. Escaped: 15% ≈ 7,290. At ₹6,500 each, about ₹4.7 crore a year. That is far more than the inspectors' cost of 12 × ₹4.2 lakh ≈ ₹50 lakh.

4. Suppose edge defects are half of all defects and the camera catches 90% of the edge defects inspectors miss. Escapes avoided ≈ 7,290 × 50% × 90% ≈ 3,280 a year, worth about ₹2.1 crore. Even at half that, the ₹1.1 crore system pays back within a year, without cutting a single inspector. Headcount changes can come later, once the system has proved itself.

What the case teaches. The money in quality is usually in escapes, not inspection labour. Framing vision AI as "catch what people miss" is more valuable, easier to prove and easier for the plant to accept than "replace people".

Generative AI looks like magic and is mostly prediction, repeated. This session bridges what you learned in this part to the three parts that follow: generative AI, agents and MCP.

A language model predicts the next token

Text is cut into tokens: pieces of words, typically three or four characters on average in English. A language model is a neural network trained on a huge amount of text to do one thing: given the tokens so far, give a probability for every possible next token. To generate, you pick a token from that distribution, add it to the text, and ask again. A paragraph is a few hundred repetitions of that loop.

How the next token is picked matters. Always taking the most likely token gives safe, repetitive text. Sampling with some randomness gives variety. The temperature setting controls this: low temperature for extraction and code, higher for brainstorming.

The transformer and attention

Older models read text one word at a time and forgot the start of long sentences. The transformer (2017) lets every token look at every other token in the context and decide which ones matter for it. That mechanism is called attention. In "the bracket on the left frame member cracked because it was undersized", attention lets "it" connect to "bracket". Stacking dozens of attention layers and training on trillions of tokens produced today's large language models.

Pretraining, then instruction tuning

A raw model trained only to predict text will continue a question rather than answer it. Models are then tuned on examples of good answers and on human preferences, which turns a text predictor into an assistant. This is why the same underlying model can follow instructions, refuse harmful requests and use tools: all of it was trained in on top of next-token prediction.

Images: diffusion

Image generators mostly use diffusion. During training, the model sees images with increasing amounts of random noise added and learns to predict the noise. To generate, it starts from pure noise and removes it step by step, guided by a text prompt. The same idea is being applied to 3D shapes and to engineering fields such as flow and stress.

Three things engineers call "generative"

ApproachHow it worksOutput
Topology optimisationPhysics-based: removes material from a design space where stress is low, under given loads and constraints. Deterministic.One organic shape per set of inputs
Generative designRuns many optimisations with different materials, manufacturing methods and goals, and presents a range of options to compare.Many candidate designs with trade-offs

Customers often mix these up. Generative design is physics plus search, and its results can be verified by simulation. Generative AI is learned from data and its output must be checked; it can be wrong in ways that look plausible.

Why generative AI is different from Part 2's models

  • It is general: one model writes, summarises, translates, extracts and reasons, without retraining.
  • You steer it with words (prompts), not with training data.
  • Its errors are fluent. A wrong answer looks as confident as a right one, so evaluation and grounding in your own documents (Part 3) matter more, not less.
  • It can be given tools and act (Part 4), connected to your systems through a standard protocol (Part 5).

You will build a word-level language model from scratch in thirty lines, generate text with different temperatures, and then see how a real model's tokenizer cuts up engineering text. No GPU needed.

Step 1: a small corpus of work instructions

corpus = """
torque the frame bolts to 45 nm in a cross pattern .
torque the axle bolts to 60 nm in a cross pattern .
inspect the weld seam for porosity before painting .
inspect the busbar joints for discolouration before sealing .
fit the tail lamp and connect the lighting harness .
fit the headlamp and connect the main harness .
check the battery pack insulation before sealing the enclosure .
check the frame weld seam for cracks before painting .
"""
words = corpus.split()
print(len(words), "words,", len(set(words)), "distinct")

Step 2: count what follows what

from collections import defaultdict, Counter

next_counts = defaultdict(Counter)
for a, b in zip(words, words[1:]):
    next_counts[a][b] += 1

print(next_counts["the"].most_common(8))
print(next_counts["before"].most_common())

This is a bigram model: it predicts the next word only from the current word. It has learned, for example, that after "before" comes "painting" or "sealing". A large language model does the same job, predicting the next token, but looks at thousands of previous tokens through attention and has learned from trillions of tokens.

Step 3: turn counts into probabilities and sample

import random

def next_word(word, temperature=1.0):
    options = next_counts[word]
    if not options:
        return "."
    items = list(options.items())
    weights = [count ** (1 / temperature) for _, count in items]
    return random.choices([w for w, _ in items], weights=weights)[0]

def generate(start, n=12, temperature=1.0, seed=None):
    random.seed(seed)
    out = [start]
    for _ in range(n):
        out.append(next_word(out[-1], temperature))
        if out[-1] == ".":
            break
    return " ".join(out)

for t in [0.2, 1.0, 3.0]:
    print(f"temperature {t}:")
    for s in range(3):
        print("   ", generate("torque", temperature=t, seed=s))

Raising counts to the power 1 ÷ temperature sharpens the distribution when the temperature is low and flattens it when high. In this tiny corpus most counts are 1, so temperature changes less than it would in a real model; after "the", though, "frame" (seen twice) wins far more often at 0.2. Now read the outputs closely. You will see sentences such as "torque the busbar joints for discolouration before sealing" or "torque the battery pack insulation before painting". Every word follows its neighbour plausibly, yet the instruction is nonsense. That is a hallucination in miniature: fluent, plausible and false. It happens for the same reason in large models: the output follows the patterns of language, not a check against reality. Higher temperatures make such jumps more frequent; lower temperatures make them rarer but never impossible.
